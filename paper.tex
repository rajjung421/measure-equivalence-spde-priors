\begin{abstract}
Learning a Gaussian prior from replicated spatial fields requires deciding not only how much flexibility to add, but where that flexibility belongs. We study whether a population covariance discrepancy can be represented as a correction of a reference Gaussian field or instead requires changing the differential operator that defines the reference. Using a leaky-aquifer SPDE, we compare finite-dimensional source variability, spatially varying leakage, and spatially varying conductivity. Gaussian-measure equivalence theory predicts that the first two remain within the reference measure class, whereas a principal-coefficient change does not. We translate this distinction into a refinement diagnostic based on reference-whitened replicated fields. For a conductivity lens, the diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N$ computed from 200 fields tracks the finite-grid oracle sequence toward the nonzero constant predicted from the principal symbol. Wrong structural classes fail systematically: source variability drives a stationary range estimate from the generating value $\kappa_0=6$ to $3\times10^{-5}$, while correction-only models incur a cost growing linearly with resolution on principal-part variation. A combined model that jointly adapts the principal operator and learns finite-rank source corrections returns $\widehat\kappa=6.08$ on the same source-only truth and recovers the conductivity field and source subspace in the mixed case. Whether to correct a Gaussian reference or change its operator is a question that replicated data can answer, and getting that decision wrong can produce a confident, physically plausible, but structurally incorrect model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Gaussian random fields are widely used as probabilistic models for spatially varying quantities, and stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) representations provide a useful connection between covariance structure and local differential operators \citep{Lindgren2011,LindgrenBolinRue2022}. When replicated realizations of a spatial field are available, they provide information about population-level covariance structure beyond that contained in a single realization. Replicated fields have therefore been used to estimate nonstationary spatial dependence and to separate persistent population structure from realization-specific variation \citep{Ingebrigtsen2015,Fuglstad2015}. This leads to the question studied here: can replicated fields reveal whether a covariance discrepancy can be represented as a correction of a chosen reference model, or whether the operator defining that reference must itself change?

A common response to mismatch is to introduce flexibility around a reference model. One class of methods augments a simpler covariance model with low-dimensional or low-rank structure, as in fixed-rank kriging, predictive processes, and full-scale covariance approximations \citep{CressieJohannesson2008,Banerjee2008,SangHuang2012}. Predictive nonstationary constructions such as LatticeKrig, nearest-neighbor Gaussian processes (NNGP), and fused Gaussian processes likewise operate on the correction side of a fixed reference and are therefore subject to the same reference-adequacy diagnostic considered here. A second class modifies the covariance-generating operator, for example by allowing spatially varying range, anisotropy, or coefficients in an SPDE representation \citep{Fuglstad2015,LindgrenBolinRue2022}. In Bayesian formulations, penalized-complexity (PC) priors provide a principled way to shrink a flexible model component toward a simpler base model \citep{Simpson2017,Fuglstad2019}. In each case the reference is taken as given; whether it is the right reference for the observed population is not asked.

For Gaussian fields in function space, departures from a reference can have different mathematical character. By the Feldman--H\'ajek dichotomy, two Gaussian measures are either equivalent or mutually singular. For generalized Whittle--Mat\'ern fields generated by elliptic operators, \citet{BolinKirchner2023} give conditions under which changes of the operator preserve or destroy equivalence. Here the \emph{principal part} is the highest-order differential term, for example $-\nabla\!\cdot\!(K(x)\nabla)$ in a second-order elliptic operator, while $\kappa^2$ is a zeroth-order term. In the dimensions considered here, changes to certain lower-order terms can remain within the same Gaussian measure class, whereas a change in the principal coefficient does not preserve equivalence: equality of the principal coefficient is required, with even a constant rescaling acting as a covariance-amplitude change. Finite-dimensional source perturbations can also remain within the reference measure class when the induced field directions satisfy the Cameron--Martin requirement. In the setting studied below, smooth source profiles satisfy this requirement, whereas idealized point sources do not. Thus source, lower-order, and principal-part perturbations can all appear as covariance changes on a finite mesh while having different continuum behavior.

This distinction is directly relevant to methods that aim to learn probability measures independently of discretization. Function-space diffusion models introduce Gaussian reference or perturbing measures precisely so that the learned construction remains meaningful as resolution changes. \citet{Lim2025} formulate score-based diffusion in function space under absolute-continuity and Cameron--Martin conditions relative to a Gaussian perturbing measure, while \citet{Pidstrigach2023} show that discretize-first diffusion constructions can deteriorate under mesh refinement and instead develop an infinite-dimensional formulation with dimension-independent guarantees. The inverse-problem setting provides a useful safe-case contrast: with finite-dimensional observations, the Gaussian posterior differs from its prior only through a finite-dimensional information update, and \citet{CarereLie2026} characterize low-rank posterior approximations that remain equivalent to both prior and posterior. Population-prior learning has no analogous guarantee that the observed population remains in the measure class of the chosen reference.

The statistical problem is therefore different from comparing two specified continuum models. In prior learning, the reference model and a finite collection of replicated realizations are observed, while the mechanism producing the covariance discrepancy is unknown. Every pair of nonsingular Gaussian distributions on a fixed finite-dimensional discretization is mutually absolutely continuous, so a single mesh does not reveal the continuum distinction directly. Classical fixed-domain results add a second difficulty: covariance parameters can be weakly identified even when prediction remains accurate, and misspecified range parameters can produce asymptotically similar interpolation behavior \citep{Stein1999,Zhang2004,KaufmanShaby2013}. In particular, the range parameter provides a weakly constrained low-frequency direction through which a stationary fit can absorb localized source variability. A finite-resolution model can therefore be statistically good while assigning variability to the wrong structural component.

The broader problem of mechanism misattribution is already recognized in spatial statistics. \citet{Fuglstad2015} show that apparent covariance nonstationarity can arise from inadequately modeled mean structure: missing spatial covariates can be absorbed by the covariance model, creating spurious long-range dependence or apparent global nonstationarity. Related work with replicated spatial fields uses repeated realizations to help separate competing sources of spatial variation \citep{Ingebrigtsen2015}. At the SPDE level, \citet{CuiHe2027} derive identifiability conditions for drift and diffusion components from trajectory data using mean and covariance structure; their problem concerns dynamical SPDE discovery rather than covariance attribution across replicated equilibrium fields. Other nearby work fixes one side of the distinction. \citet{Bolin2026} places known covariates into the forcing of an SPDE to separate pointwise and operator-mediated mean effects, while \citet{PeterssonSchroers2025} develops estimation and goodness-of-fit tests for the driving-noise covariance with the differential operator treated as given. The question considered here is instead whether a covariance discrepancy across replicated fields should be assigned to the source covariance or to the spatial operator generating the field, and how those alternatives behave under mesh refinement.

This distinction also matters for reference-based prior construction. PC priors formalize model complexity as departure from a chosen base model \citep{Simpson2017}. For Mat\'ern fields, the construction is parameterized so that the relevant distance from the base remains meaningful \citep{Fuglstad2019}. More recent work on anisotropic SPDEs shows that principal-operator changes can produce mutually singular Gaussian measures, so alternative complexity measures are required when the usual Kullback--Leibler construction is unavailable \citep{LlamazaresElias2026}. These results motivate a question that precedes regularization around a base model: is the chosen reference operator adequate for the observed population? A within-class departure can be represented and regularized relative to that reference, whereas principal-part variation requires adaptation of the operator defining the reference covariance. On a fixed mesh, forcing a principal-part change into a reference-based correction can still produce a finite KL discrepancy, but that discrepancy can grow with resolution, making the implied complexity itself discretization dependent.

We study this question using a leaky-aquifer SPDE as a controlled physical setting because uncertain pumping, spatially varying leakage, and heterogeneous conductivity enter respectively through the source, a lower-order term, and the principal part of the operator. Let $\Sigma_{0,N}$ denote the covariance of the nominal reference model on a discretization with $N$ degrees of freedom, and let $\Sigma_{1,N}$ denote the population covariance of the field-generating process on the same discretization. We define the relative covariance departure in reference-whitened coordinates by
\begin{equation}
    T_N
    =
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    \Sigma_{1,N}
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    - I .
    \label{eq:relative_covariance}
\end{equation}
The population covariance $\Sigma_{1,N}$ is unknown in practice. Given replicated fields $u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(n)}$, it is replaced by the sample covariance $\widehat{\Sigma}_{1,N}$, giving
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{T}_N
    =
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    \widehat{\Sigma}_{1,N}
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    - I .
    \label{eq:empirical_relative_covariance}
\end{equation}
The continuum theory characterizes the limiting structure of $T_N$; the statistical problem is to infer that structure from $\widehat{T}_N$ at finite sample size and finite resolution. We use its spectral and refinement behavior to distinguish source-like covariance corrections from persistent principal-part discrepancy and to study the failures produced by assigning the discrepancy to the wrong structural component.

The resulting modeling principle is to adapt the reference operator when the replicated population exhibits principal-part variation and to represent finite-dimensional source variability as a correction relative to the adapted reference. We implement this principle in a combined model that learns a spatially varying principal coefficient together with a low-rank covariance correction. The operator component and correction component are selected separately from the data, so source-like and operator-level variability need not imitate one another.

The contributions of this work are fourfold. First, we use established Gaussian-measure equivalence results to formulate a statistical prior-learning problem that distinguishes source, lower-order, and principal-part departures through their behavior under discretization refinement (Section~\ref{sec:results_oracle}). Second, we develop a sample-based whitening and refinement diagnostic for persistent principal-part mismatch and evaluate how its finite-sample behavior changes when the departure is not separated from the sample covariance bulk as an isolated eigenvalue (Section~\ref{sec:results_sample}). Third, we characterize asymmetric mechanism-misattribution failures when source variability is represented through the operator or principal-part variability is represented through a correction-only model; we also show that the KL cost of the latter can grow with resolution, linking structural misspecification to discretization-dependent reference-based complexity (Section~\ref{sec:results_misattribution}). Fourth, we introduce a combined model that learns the principal operator and finite-dimensional source corrections jointly, allowing each form of variability to be represented by the component suited to its structure (Section~\ref{sec:results_m5}).

\section{Problem formulation and theoretical basis}
\label{sec:theory}

This section defines the population-level distinction that will later be inferred from finite replicated data. The aim is to identify what separates a covariance correction that remains in the Gaussian measure class of a reference field from a departure that changes the covariance-generating operator itself.

\subsection{Reference field and population-learning problem}
\label{sec:reference}

Let $\mathcal D\subset\mathbb R^2$ be a bounded domain and consider the elliptic SPDE
\begin{equation}
    A_0 u = \mathcal W,
    \qquad
    A_0
    =
    \kappa_0^2 I
    -
    \nabla\!\cdot\!\left(K_0\nabla\right),
    \label{eq:reference_spde}
\end{equation}
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. Here $\mathcal W$ denotes spatial Gaussian white noise, $\kappa_0^2$ is a zeroth-order coefficient, and $K_0$ is the principal coefficient. The nominal reference used below has $K_0\equiv1$. At the continuum level, we take $\mathcal W$ to have identity covariance in the formal white-noise coordinates. Under the present boundary conditions $A_0$ is positive and self-adjoint, so the centered reference field has law
\begin{equation}
    \mu_0
    =
    \mathcal N(0,\Sigma_0),
    \qquad
    \Sigma_0
    =
    A_0^{-1}A_0^{-1}
    =
    A_0^{-2}.
    \label{eq:reference_covariance}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:reference_covariance} is a continuum operator identity. Its discrete form depends on the representation of white noise. For the finite-difference scheme used in the numerical study, nodal white noise has covariance $Q_W=h^{-2}I_N$, giving $\Sigma_{0,N}=\sigma^2h^{-2}A_{0,N}^{-1}A_{0,N}^{-T}$. The complete discretization is specified in Section~\ref{sec:numerical_design}.

The value $\kappa_0=0$ remains admissible on a bounded domain with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions. In that case $A_0=-\Delta_D$, whose smallest eigenvalue is strictly positive, so the inverse and the covariance remain well defined. This observation will matter when fitting stationary reference models, because decreasing $\kappa$ corresponds to increasing the spatial range without making the bounded-domain covariance singular at $\kappa=0$.

We assume that replicated fields
\begin{equation}
    u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(n)}
    \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}
    \mu_1
    =
    \mathcal N(0,\Sigma_1)
    \label{eq:population_replicates}
\end{equation}
are generated from an unknown population law. The prior-learning problem is to determine how $\Sigma_1$ differs structurally from the known reference covariance $\Sigma_0$. In particular, the population departure may arise through the stochastic source, through a lower-order term in the operator, or through the principal coefficient itself.

\subsection{Relative covariance in reference-whitened coordinates}
\label{sec:relative_covariance}

The comparison becomes transparent after removing the covariance already represented by the reference. Define
\begin{equation}
    T
    =
    \Sigma_0^{-1/2}
    \Sigma_1
    \Sigma_0^{-1/2}
    - I .
    \label{eq:T_continuum}
\end{equation}
If
\begin{equation}
    z=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}u,
\end{equation}
then the reference field satisfies $\operatorname{Cov}_{\mu_0}(z)=I$, while under the population law
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}_{\mu_1}(z)=I+T.
\end{equation}
Thus $T$ is the covariance structure that remains after whitening by the reference model.

On a discretization with $N$ degrees of freedom, the corresponding matrix is
\begin{equation}
    T_N
    =
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    \Sigma_{1,N}
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    - I_N .
    \label{eq:T_discrete}
\end{equation}
Its eigenvalues $t_{j,N}$ quantify relative covariance departures mode by mode. The accumulated squared relative departure is
\begin{equation}
    D_N
    :=
    \|T_N\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
    =
    \sum_{j=1}^{N} t_{j,N}^2 .
    \label{eq:Dn}
\end{equation}
The refinement behavior of $D_N$ will provide the bridge between continuum equivalence and the finite-dimensional statistics used later.

\subsection{Gaussian equivalence and the Hilbert--Schmidt condition}
\label{sec:fh}

For two Gaussian measures on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, the Feldman--H\'ajek theorem gives an equivalence-or-singularity dichotomy. In one standard formulation, equivalence requires: (i) equality of the Cameron--Martin spaces, (ii) the mean difference to lie in that common space, and (iii) an appropriate relative covariance operator to be Hilbert--Schmidt \citep[Thm.~2.25]{DaPratoZabczyk2014}. All fields considered here are centered, so the mean condition is automatic. For the elliptic families below, the common-domain and Cameron--Martin conditions are supplied by the corresponding operator results; the condition that distinguishes the cases of interest is therefore the Hilbert--Schmidt behavior of the relative covariance.

Under these conditions,
\begin{equation}
    T\in\mathcal S_2
    \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
    \|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
    =
    \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}t_j^2
    <\infty ,
    \label{eq:HScriterion}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal S_2$ denotes the Hilbert--Schmidt class. We use this condition to distinguish departures whose cumulative relative effect decays sufficiently rapidly across fine scales from departures that persist through the high-frequency spectrum.

\subsection{Three forms of population departure}
\label{sec:three_departures}

The leaky-aquifer example provides three physically distinct perturbations of \eqref{eq:reference_spde}: uncertainty in pumping acts through the source, spatially varying leakage changes the zeroth-order term, and heterogeneous conductivity changes the principal part.

\subsubsection{Source variability}

Let the source contain $r$ random smooth profiles,
\begin{equation}
    A_0u
    =
    \mathcal W
    +
    G\xi,
    \qquad
    G\xi
    =
    \sum_{k=1}^{r}\xi_k g_k,
    \qquad
    \xi\sim\mathcal N(0,C).
    \label{eq:source_departure}
\end{equation}
The resulting covariance is
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_1
    =
    A_0^{-1}
    \left(I+GCG^\ast\right)
    A_0^{-1}.
    \label{eq:source_covariance}
\end{equation}
Using the continuum identity $\Sigma_0^{1/2}=A_0^{-1}$, whitening gives
\begin{equation}
    T
    =
    GCG^\ast .
    \label{eq:source_T}
\end{equation}
Hence $\operatorname{rank}(T)\le r$ and $T$ is Hilbert--Schmidt.

For $\mu_0=\mathcal N(0,A_0^{-2})$, the Cameron--Martin space is $\operatorname{Ran}(A_0^{-1})$. The induced field direction $A_0^{-1}g_k$ therefore belongs to the Cameron--Martin space exactly when the forcing profile satisfies $g_k\in L^2(\mathcal D)$. Smooth well profiles satisfy this condition, whereas idealized delta sources do not. The smooth-source assumption is therefore load-bearing for the finite-rank in-class construction.

\subsubsection{Spatially varying leakage}

Let the principal coefficient remain fixed while the zeroth-order coefficient changes,
\begin{equation}
    A_1
    =
    -\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(K_0\nabla\right)
    +
    \kappa^2(x).
    \label{eq:leakage_operator}
\end{equation}
For the smooth setting covered by \citet[Thm.~4.11 and Table~1]{BolinKirchner2023}, changes of this lower-order coefficient remain within the same Gaussian measure class in the dimensional and regularity regime considered here. The corresponding relative covariance perturbation is therefore Hilbert--Schmidt, although it is generally not finite rank.

The high-frequency mechanism can be seen from a first-order expansion. Write
\begin{equation}
    A_1=A_0+V,
    \qquad
    V=\kappa^2(x)-\kappa_0^2,
\end{equation}
where $V$ denotes multiplication by the leakage perturbation. To first order,
\begin{equation}
    T
    \approx
    -
    \left(
        V A_0^{-1}
        +
        A_0^{-1}V
    \right).
    \label{eq:leakage_first_order}
\end{equation}
The exact equivalence statement is supplied by the operator theorem; \eqref{eq:leakage_first_order} gives the high-frequency mechanism. For bounded smooth $V$, the singular values of $A_0^{-1}V$ inherit the order-$-2$ elliptic decay
\begin{equation}
    s_j(A_0^{-1}V)=O\!\left(j^{-2/d}\right).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_j s_j(A_0^{-1}V)^2
    \sim
    \sum_j j^{-4/d}
\end{equation}
is summable for $d<4$. Thus a lower-order perturbation can affect infinitely many modes while its relative effect decays rapidly enough to remain Hilbert--Schmidt in $d\le3$.

\subsubsection{Spatially varying conductivity}

Now let the principal coefficient change,
\begin{equation}
    A_1
    =
    \kappa_0^2 I
    -
    \nabla\!\cdot\!\left(K_1(x)\nabla\right).
    \label{eq:conductivity_operator}
\end{equation}
For the variable-coefficient setting of \citet[Thm.~4.11 and Lemma~4.9]{BolinKirchner2023}, equivalence requires equality of the principal coefficient:
\begin{equation}
    K_1(x)=K_0(x).
    \label{eq:principal_equality}
\end{equation}
A nontrivial change in $K(x)$ therefore changes the Gaussian measure class. With the driving-noise normalization held fixed, even a constant rescaling $K_1=cK_0$, $c\neq1$, acts as an amplitude change and does not preserve equivalence relative to the fixed reference. For the stationary Laplacian case this is seen directly from
\begin{equation}
    \left(\kappa^2-c\Delta\right)^{-2}
    =
    c^{-2}
    \left(\frac{\kappa^2}{c}-\Delta\right)^{-2},
    \label{eq:constant_rescaling}
\end{equation}
so a constant principal-coefficient rescaling changes both covariance amplitude and range rather than leaving the Gaussian reference unchanged.

The three departures are summarized as
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Physical departure & SPDE location & Relative covariance & Model component\\
\midrule
Random smooth pumping & Source & Finite rank; in class & Low-rank correction\\
Spatially varying leakage & Zeroth order & HS; generally infinite rank ($d\le3$) & Lower-order operator\\
Spatially varying conductivity & Principal part & Outside reference class & Principal operator\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The variable-coefficient theorem invoked above assumes smooth coefficients and a smooth boundary. The main conductivity lens used below is smooth, so the coefficient regularity is consistent with that setting; the unit-square domain, however, has corners and is not a smooth boundary. We therefore test whether the predicted refinement signature persists on the polygonal domain. A discontinuous disc lens is considered separately as a robustness experiment.

\subsection{Refinement behavior of the relative covariance}
\label{sec:refinement_criterion}

The Hilbert--Schmidt condition gives an immediate finite-resolution consequence for in-class departures.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded refinement energy for an in-class departure]
\label{prop:refinement}
Let $\{P_N\}_{N\ge1}$ be nested finite-rank orthogonal projections converging strongly to the identity, let $T\in\mathcal S_2$, and define
\begin{equation}
    T_N=P_NTP_N,
    \qquad
    D_N=\|T_N\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
    D_N
    \longrightarrow
    \|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
    <\infty,
    \qquad
    \frac{D_N}{N}\longrightarrow0.
    \label{eq:prop_hs}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof_refinement}. Proposition~\ref{prop:refinement} formalizes the refinement signature of an in-class departure: its accumulated squared relative covariance approaches a finite limit.

For a principal-coefficient change, \citet[Lemma~4.9]{BolinKirchner2023} establish the non-compactness that underlies failure of equivalence. Non-compactness implies that the relative perturbation does not decay in the manner required of a Hilbert--Schmidt operator, but it does not by itself imply a linear growth rate for $D_N$. The stronger scaling
\begin{equation}
    D_N=O(N),
    \qquad
    \frac{D_N}{N}\longrightarrow c>0,
    \label{eq:extensive_scaling}
\end{equation}
follows from a persistent-mode mechanism: if there exist $\rho,\delta>0$ such that at least $\rho N$ eigenvalues satisfy $|t_{j,N}|\ge\delta$, then
\begin{equation}
    D_N
    =
    \sum_{j=1}^{N}t_{j,N}^2
    \ge
    \rho N\delta^2.
    \label{eq:persistent_mode_bound}
\end{equation}
For a smooth principal-coefficient change, the following lemma shows that this mechanism is present and identifies the limits.

\begin{lemma}[A principal-part departure is multiplication plus compact]
\label{lem:mult_compact}
Let $\mathcal D\subset\mathbb R^2$ be bounded with either a smooth boundary or a convex polygonal boundary, let $K\in C^1(\overline{\mathcal D})$ with $K\ge k_0>0$, and let
\begin{equation}
    A_0=\kappa^2I-\Delta,
    \qquad
    A_1=\kappa^2I-\nabla\!\cdot(K\nabla),
\end{equation}
with the same homogeneous Dirichlet conditions and $\Sigma_j=c\,A_j^{-2}$ for a common constant $c>0$. Then
\begin{equation}
    T=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{-1/2}-I=M_m+R,
    \qquad
    m(x)=K(x)^{-2}-1,
    \label{eq:mult_compact}
\end{equation}
where $M_m$ is multiplication by $m$ and $R$ is compact on $L^2(\mathcal D)$. Consequently:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] $\sigma_{\rm ess}(T)=m(\overline{\mathcal D})$. In particular $T$ is not compact unless $K\equiv1$.
    \item[(ii)] Let $P_N$ project onto piecewise-constant functions on a partition of $\mathcal D$ into $N$ cells of equal measure whose diameters tend to zero, and let $T_N=P_NTP_N$. Then
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{D_N}{N}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{|\mathcal D|}\int_{\mathcal D}m^2\,dx,
        \qquad
        \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(T_N)_{ii}^2\longrightarrow\frac{1}{|\mathcal D|}\int_{\mathcal D}m^2\,dx,
        \label{eq:lemma_energy}
    \end{equation}
    so the off-diagonal energy per node vanishes.
    \item[(iii)] The empirical eigenvalue distribution of $T_N$ converges weakly to the distribution of $m(X)$ with $X$ uniform on $\mathcal D$. Hence the persistent-mode condition holds with $\rho=|\{|m|\ge\delta\}|/|\mathcal D|$, and
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\mathrm{KL}_N}{N}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2|\mathcal D|}\int_{\mathcal D}\big[m-\log(1+m)\big]\,dx.
        \label{eq:lemma_kl}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof_lemma}. Part (i) recovers the non-compactness of \citet[Lemma~4.9]{BolinKirchner2023}; parts (ii) and (iii) give the extensive scaling \eqref{eq:extensive_scaling} with an explicit constant, which equivalence theory alone does not provide. The lemma is stated for a piecewise-constant Galerkin compression; the finite-difference relative covariance used in the numerical study is a consistent approximation of it, and the agreement of the two is checked numerically in Section~\ref{sec:results_oracle}. The proof requires $K\in C^1$ and does not cover the discontinuous disc lens of Section~\ref{sec:robustness}; there the per-node energy also persists ($D_N/N\approx0.035$), which suggests that the conclusion extends, but this is not proved here.

The same distinction appears in Gaussian relative entropy. If the eigenvalues of $I+T_N$ remain bounded away from zero, then
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{KL}_N(\mu_1\,\|\,\mu_0)
    =
    \frac12
    \sum_{j=1}^{N}
    \left[
        t_{j,N}
        -
        \log(1+t_{j,N})
    \right].
    \label{eq:gaussian_kl}
\end{equation}
For a bounded Hilbert--Schmidt perturbation this sum can converge, whereas a persistent $O(1)$ shift over a positive fraction of modes produces an $O(N)$ KL cost; for a smooth principal-coefficient change, Lemma~\ref{lem:mult_compact}(iii) gives its slope. Thus a principal-part mismatch can appear finite at every fixed discretization while becoming increasingly distant from the reference as the mesh is refined.

\subsection{What the continuum theory does not determine}
\label{sec:handoff}

The results above compare known population covariances. They assume access to
\begin{equation}
    T_N
    =
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    \Sigma_{1,N}
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    - I_N
\end{equation}
along a refinement sequence. In the prior-learning problem, however, $\Sigma_{1,N}$ is unknown. At a given resolution, the replicated fields provide only the sample covariance
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\Sigma}_{1,N}
    =
    \frac{1}{n}
    \sum_{i=1}^{n}
    u^{(i)}u^{(i)\top}
\end{equation}
for centered data, and hence the empirical relative covariance
\begin{equation}
    \widehat T_N
    =
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    \widehat{\Sigma}_{1,N}
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    - I_N.
    \label{eq:T_hat_sec2}
\end{equation}
When $N/n$ is not small, the spectrum and norm of $\widehat T_N$ are themselves strongly affected by sampling variability. Under the exact reference model, let
\begin{equation}
    z_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,I_N),
    \qquad
    S=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_i z_i^\top .
\end{equation}
Even though the population relative covariance is zero,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E\|S-I_N\|_F^2
    =
    \frac{N(N+1)}{n}.
    \label{eq:wishart_inflation}
\end{equation}
Thus the naive sample Hilbert--Schmidt norm grows with dimension under the null and cannot be used directly as a refinement diagnostic. The continuum theory therefore identifies \emph{what} must be distinguished, but not \emph{how} to distinguish it from a finite ensemble of fields. The next section develops the sample-based diagnostics and covariance models used to make that distinction.

\section{Statistical methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

Section~\ref{sec:theory} identifies the continuum distinction to be inferred: a Hilbert--Schmidt relative covariance departure has bounded accumulated refinement energy, whereas a persistent principal-part departure can retain non-vanishing relative covariance energy per resolved degree of freedom. In practice, however, the population covariance is unavailable and the sample covariance is strongly contaminated by finite-ensemble fluctuations. We therefore separate the statistical problem into two tasks: first diagnose the structure of the departure from replicated fields, and then fit a covariance model whose components are capable of representing that structure.

\subsection{Diagnosing covariance departure from finite replicates}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

\subsubsection{Estimated reference and whitening}
\label{sec:estimated_reference}

Let
\begin{equation}
    \widehat\Sigma_{0,N}
    =
    \Sigma_{0,N}(\widehat\sigma^2,\widehat\kappa)
\end{equation}
denote the stationary reference covariance obtained by maximum likelihood from the replicated fields. We use this fitted stationary model as a \emph{reference for whitening}, not as the final covariance model. If the discrete reference covariance is written in the general form
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{0,N}
    =
    \sigma^2 A_\kappa^{-1}Q_W A_\kappa^{-T},
\end{equation}
with $Q_W$ the discrete white-noise covariance, a corresponding whitening map is
\begin{equation}
    z^{(j)}
    =
    \widehat\sigma^{-1}
    Q_W^{-1/2}
    A_{\widehat\kappa}u^{(j)},
    \qquad
    j=1,\ldots,n.
    \label{eq:sample_whitening}
\end{equation}
For the finite-difference discretization used in the numerical study, $Q_W=h^{-2}I$ and \eqref{eq:sample_whitening} reduces to $z=hA_{\widehat\kappa}u/\widehat\sigma$.

The whitened sample covariance is
\begin{equation}
    S
    =
    \frac1n
    \sum_{j=1}^{n}
    z^{(j)}z^{(j)\top}.
    \label{eq:whitened_sample_cov}
\end{equation}
If the reference parameters were known, $\mathbb E[S]=I+T_N$. Because the same replicated fields are used to estimate $(\widehat\sigma^2,\widehat\kappa)$, the resulting diagnostic is a plug-in statistic rather than an exactly unbiased estimate of the relative covariance. This distinction is relevant when the stationary range parameter is weakly identified or moves toward the boundary. Such range misspecification remains an in-class covariance deformation, however, and therefore cannot by itself generate a nonzero per-node refinement plateau of the type sought below. We assess the finite-sample sensitivity of the diagnostic to reference fitting separately in the numerical study.

\subsubsection{Spectral outliers and finite-dimensional source structure}
\label{sec:spectral_diag}

A finite-rank source perturbation produces isolated population eigenvalues in the whitened covariance,
\begin{equation}
    I+T
    =
    I
    +
    \sum_{k=1}^{r}c_kv_kv_k^\top,
    \qquad c_k>0 .
\end{equation}
At finite $N$ and $n$, even the null covariance produces a nontrivial sample spectrum. Let
\begin{equation}
    \gamma=\frac{N}{n}.
\end{equation}
For whitened Gaussian noise, the upper Marchenko--Pastur edge is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_+
    =
    \left(1+\sqrt{\gamma}\right)^2 .
    \label{eq:mp_edge}
\end{equation}
We therefore report the spectral-outlier count
\begin{equation}
    \widehat r_{\mathrm{MP}}
    =
    \#\left\{
    \widehat s_k>\lambda_+
    \right\},
    \label{eq:rmp}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat s_k$ are the nonzero eigenvalues of $S$ in decreasing order. The count is used as a diagnostic for isolated positive covariance directions; the fitted correction rank is selected independently by cross-validation.

The outlier count alone cannot diagnose a principal-part departure. A spatially distributed operator change can alter many population modes without producing an isolated sample eigenvalue outside the Marchenko--Pastur bulk. This motivates a second statistic that accumulates a local signature over the spatial discretization.

\subsubsection{Diagonal refinement statistic $h_N$}
\label{sec:diag_stat}

Let
\begin{equation}
    d_i
    =
    \frac1n
    \sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{ij}^2
    \label{eq:di}
\end{equation}
denote the empirical variance of the whitened field at node $i$. For a fixed whitening reference,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[d_i]
    =
    1+T_{ii}.
\end{equation}
Under Gaussian sampling,
\begin{equation}
    d_i
    \overset{d}{=}
    (1+T_{ii})\frac{\chi_n^2}{n},
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{align}
    \mathbb E\left[(d_i-1)^2\right]
    &=
    T_{ii}^2
    +
    \frac{2}{n}(1+T_{ii})^2,\\
    \mathbb E[d_i^2]
    &=
    \frac{n+2}{n}(1+T_{ii})^2.
\end{align}
This gives the bias-corrected statistic
\begin{equation}
    \widehat H_N
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left[
        (d_i-1)^2
        -
        \frac{2}{n+2}d_i^2
    \right],
    \label{eq:Hhat}
\end{equation}
for which, conditional on a fixed whitening reference,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[\widehat H_N]
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{ii}^2.
    \label{eq:H_unbiased}
\end{equation}
We use the normalized quantity
\begin{equation}
    \widehat h_N
    =
    \frac{\widehat H_N}{N}.
    \label{eq:hhat}
\end{equation}

The statistic is motivated by the distinction in Section~\ref{sec:theory}. If $T$ is Hilbert--Schmidt, then for any orthonormal basis
\begin{equation}
    \sum_i T_{ii}^2
    \le
    \|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
    <\infty,
\end{equation}
so the corresponding per-node diagonal energy vanishes under refinement. A principal-coefficient change has a different local high-frequency signature. By Lemma~\ref{lem:mult_compact}, the relative covariance of a smooth scalar conductivity perturbation is multiplication by
\begin{equation}
    m(x)
    =
    K(x)^{-2}-1
\end{equation}
plus a compact remainder. Consequently, under regular refinement (Lemma~\ref{lem:mult_compact}(ii)),
\begin{equation}
    \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{ii}^2
    \longrightarrow
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal D|}
    \int_{\mathcal D}
    \left[K(x)^{-2}-1\right]^2\,dx
    =:
    h_\infty,
    \label{eq:hlimit}
\end{equation}
for the piecewise-constant compression of the lemma; for the finite-difference grid basis used here the limit is a prediction checked against the oracle values. For the conductivity lens specified in Section~\ref{sec:numerical_design}, $h_\infty=0.060732$, and the finite-grid oracle values approach it from above; the sample-based estimate is compared with both in the Results section.

Equation~\eqref{eq:H_unbiased} is exact only when the whitening reference is fixed. Estimating the stationary reference from the same replicates introduces plug-in error, and estimating and removing source directions from the same sample introduces an additional dependence. We therefore refer to \eqref{eq:Hhat} as a bias-corrected diagnostic rather than an exactly unbiased test after fitting.

\subsubsection{Deflating isolated source directions}
\label{sec:deflation}

Mixed populations can contain both finite-dimensional source variability and a distributed principal-part departure. To expose the latter, we optionally remove isolated positive spectral directions before evaluating \eqref{eq:Hhat}. Let
\begin{equation}
    \widehat V_r
    =
    [\widehat v_1,\ldots,\widehat v_r]
\end{equation}
contain the sample eigenvectors associated with eigenvalues above the upper Marchenko--Pastur edge. The whitened fields are projected onto the orthogonal complement,
\begin{equation}
    Z_\perp
    =
    (I-\widehat V_r\widehat V_r^\top)Z.
    \label{eq:deflate_Z}
\end{equation}
Projection reduces the marginal variance at node $i$ by the leverage
\begin{equation}
    \ell_i
    =
    \sum_{k=1}^{r}\widehat v_{ik}^2.
\end{equation}
The deflated nodal variance is therefore renormalized as
\begin{equation}
    d_i^\perp
    =
    \frac{
        n^{-1}\sum_j z_{ij,\perp}^{\,2}
    }{
        1-\ell_i
    },
    \label{eq:deflated_di}
\end{equation}
and substituted into \eqref{eq:Hhat}.

In the experiments considered here, random sources add positive covariance directions while the conductivity lens is \emph{stiff}, $K(x)\ge1$, so its leading relative-covariance contribution is non-positive. Under this sign structure, upper-edge outliers provide a clean way to identify source directions before evaluating the principal-part diagnostic. For a soft lens, $K(x)<1$, a principal-part change can itself create positive high-frequency variance; the upper-edge count is then not mechanism-specific, although the diagonal refinement statistic $h_N$ remains sign-insensitive. The present deflation rule should therefore be interpreted as a source-removal step for the sign structure studied here, not as a universal classifier of all principal-part perturbations.

Because $\widehat V_r$ is estimated from the same sample used to compute $d_i^\perp$, the deflated statistic is only approximately bias corrected. A split-sample or cross-fitted implementation can remove this dependence and is used as a robustness check when evaluating the diagnostic.

\subsection{Covariance model classes}
\label{sec:model_classes}

The diagnostics above characterize two distinct scales of covariance departure: isolated spectral directions associated with finite-dimensional source structure, and persistent local covariance energy associated with principal-part variation. We compare five model families that place flexibility at different levels of the SPDE.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
Model & Flexible component & Intended departure & Role\\
\hline
M1 & $(\sigma^2,\kappa)$ & stationary reference & baseline\\
M2 & stationary modal spectrum & stationary misspecification & control\\
M3 & finite-rank whitened covariance & random sources & correction\\
M4-$\kappa$ & spatial zeroth-order field & leakage & lower-order operator\\
M4-$K$ & spatial principal coefficient & conductivity & principal operator\\
M5 & M4-$K$ + finite-rank correction & mixed source/operator & combined\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{M1: stationary reference}

The stationary model has covariance
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{\mathrm{M1}}
    =
    \sigma^2
    A_\kappa^{-1}
    Q_W
    A_\kappa^{-T},
    \qquad
    A_\kappa
    =
    \kappa^2I+L.
    \label{eq:M1}
\end{equation}
It defines the fitted reference used for whitening and provides the baseline against which all flexible models are compared.

\subsubsection{M2: stationary spectral reshaping}

M2 retains the eigenvectors of the stationary Laplacian but allows a smooth deformation of its modal variances. If $\lambda_j$ are the eigenvalues of $L$,
\begin{equation}
    s_j
    =
    \frac{\sigma^2}{h^2(\kappa^2+\lambda_j)^2}
    \exp[-\delta(\lambda_j)],
    \qquad
    \delta(\lambda)
    =
    \sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_q\psi_q(\log\lambda),
    \label{eq:M2}
\end{equation}
where $\psi_q$ are cubic B-spline basis functions. M2 serves as a flexible stationary control: it tests whether reshaping the stationary spectrum alone is sufficient without introducing spatially varying operator coefficients.

\subsubsection{M3: finite-rank correction}

M3 augments a stationary SPDE base with a finite-rank correction in whitened coordinates,
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{\mathrm{M3}}
    =
    \sigma^2
    A_\kappa^{-1}
    Q_W^{1/2}
    \left(
        I+VCV^\top
    \right)
    Q_W^{1/2}
    A_\kappa^{-T},
    \label{eq:M3}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    V^\top V=I,
    \qquad
    C=\operatorname{diag}(c_1,\ldots,c_r),
    \qquad c_k\ge0.
\end{equation}
For a fixed base, the maximum-likelihood correction directions are the leading eigenvectors of the whitened sample covariance, with spike strengths obtained from the corresponding excess variances. The stationary base parameters and the correction are alternated rather than fitted sequentially, because a stationary range parameter can absorb part of the same low-frequency variance that would otherwise appear as a finite-rank source component.

\subsubsection{M4-$\kappa$: lower-order adaptation}

The leakage model allows the zeroth-order coefficient to vary spatially,
\begin{equation}
    A_\theta
    =
    L
    +
    \operatorname{diag}\left\{
        \kappa^2
        \exp[\Phi\theta]
    \right\},
    \label{eq:M4kappa_operator}
\end{equation}
where the columns of $\Phi$ are smooth radial basis functions. Its covariance is
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{\mathrm{M4}\text{-}\kappa}
    =
    \sigma^2
    A_\theta^{-1}
    Q_W
    A_\theta^{-T}.
    \label{eq:M4kappa}
\end{equation}
This model directly represents the in-class lower-order departure considered in Section~\ref{sec:three_departures}.

\subsubsection{M4-$K$: principal-part adaptation}

The conductivity model changes the principal coefficient,
\begin{equation}
    A_\theta
    =
    \kappa^2I
    +
    L_{K_\theta},
    \qquad
    \log K_\theta(x)
    =
    \sum_{q=1}^{Q_K}\theta_q\phi_q(x),
    \label{eq:M4K_operator}
\end{equation}
giving
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{\mathrm{M4}\text{-}K}
    =
    \sigma^2
    A_\theta^{-1}
    Q_W
    A_\theta^{-T}.
    \label{eq:M4K}
\end{equation}
M4-$K$ changes the reference operator itself and is therefore the model class capable of representing spatially varying principal-part structure.

\subsubsection{M5: operator adaptation with finite-rank correction}

The combined model places a finite-rank correction on top of an adapted principal operator,
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \Sigma_{\mathrm{M5}}
    =
    \sigma^2
    A_\theta^{-1}
    Q_W^{1/2}
    \left(
        I+VCV^\top
    \right)
    Q_W^{1/2}
    A_\theta^{-T}
    }
    \label{eq:M5}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    A_\theta
    =
    \kappa^2I+L_{K_\theta}.
\end{equation}
The two components play different structural roles. The operator $A_\theta$ represents population variability that changes the principal spatial mechanism, while $VCV^\top$ represents finite-dimensional source variability relative to the adapted operator. M5 therefore implements the rule suggested by Section~\ref{sec:theory}: change the reference operator where the population requires a principal-part change, and represent isolated source variability as a correction around that reference.

\subsection{Estimation and model selection}
\label{sec:fitting}

All model parameters are estimated from the training replicates by likelihood or penalized likelihood. The scale parameter $\sigma^2$ is profiled analytically whenever possible.

For M1 and M2, stationarity makes the covariance diagonal in the discrete sine-transform basis, so likelihood evaluation reduces to a sum over modal variances. M4-$\kappa$ and M4-$K$ are fitted by minimizing the penalized negative log likelihood
\begin{equation}
    -\ell(\theta,\kappa,\sigma^2)
    +
    \lambda_\theta\|\theta\|_2^2,
    \label{eq:M4_penalty}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma^2$ profiled out and gradients computed by automatic differentiation.

For M3 at a fixed rank $r$, fitting alternates between two conditional updates. Given the stationary base, the fields are whitened and the leading covariance directions and strengths are updated from the whitened sample covariance. Given the correction directions and strengths, $(\sigma^2,\kappa)$ are updated by maximizing the Gaussian likelihood. The fitted rank is selected from
\begin{equation}
    r\in\{0,\ldots,r_{\max}\}
\end{equation}
using five-fold held-out log likelihood. The Marchenko--Pastur count $\widehat r_{\mathrm{MP}}$ is reported as a diagnostic only; it does not determine the fitted rank.

M5 uses exact block coordinate ascent at the level of the adapted reference. For fixed $(\kappa,\theta)$, the whitened spike block is updated by the PPCA closed form with a free residual variance, jointly updating $\sigma^2$, the spike directions, and their strengths. For fixed spike block, $(\kappa,\theta)$ are updated by penalized likelihood with $\sigma^2$ profiled in the operator objective. Each accepted block update is non-decreasing in the penalized objective, and cycles continue until the per-cycle tolerance is reached. The rank is again chosen by five-fold cross-validation. The optimization is initialized from a flat conductivity field rather than from the M4-$K$ solution so that the combined model does not inherit operator structure that M4-$K$ may have used to explain source variability.

\subsection{Evaluated decision logic}
\label{sec:decision_logic}

The diagnostic and covariance models are evaluated as complementary parts of the prior-learning problem rather than as a fully automated model-selection algorithm. The spectral and refinement diagnostics have different roles:
\begin{equation}
    \text{isolated upper spectral outliers}
    \quad\longrightarrow\quad
    \text{finite-dimensional source structure},
\end{equation}
whereas
\begin{equation}
    \widehat h_N
    \not\longrightarrow0
    \quad\longrightarrow\quad
    \text{persistent principal-part discrepancy}.
\end{equation}
This gives the structural logic
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Spectral outliers & Deflated $\widehat h_N$ & Corresponding model structure\\
\hline
present & $\to0$ & finite-rank correction (M3)\\
absent & non-vanishing & principal-operator adaptation (M4-$K$)\\
present & non-vanishing & combined adaptation and correction (M5)\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A vanishing principal-part diagnostic does not by itself distinguish a stationary field from a weak lower-order perturbation; the M4-$\kappa$ comparison is retained for that purpose.

The oracle refinement calculations and their primary predictions were specified before development of the sample-based diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N$ and the combined M5 model. The latter were introduced after the initial oracle study to address the finite-sample decision problem and the mixed source/operator case, and are evaluated separately in the subsequent numerical experiments and robustness checks.

\section{Numerical study design}
\label{sec:numerical_design}

The numerical study is designed to separate the structural question posed in Sections~\ref{sec:theory}--\ref{sec:methods} from the finite-sample fitting problem. All experiments use the same reference operator and differ only in the component through which the population departs from that reference. We first define the discretization and four synthetic populations, then distinguish the oracle refinement calculations from the finite-replicate diagnostic and model-fitting experiments. Results are deferred to Section~5.

\subsection{Domain, discretization, and reference process}
\label{sec:design_setup}

The common numerical setup is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:setup}. The domain is discretized on $n_x\times n_x$ interior nodes with spacing $h=1/(n_x+1)$. The operator $-\nabla\!\cdot(K\nabla u)$ is approximated by a five-point finite-difference stencil; for heterogeneous $K$, conductance across an edge uses the harmonic mean of the adjacent nodal values. Homogeneous Dirichlet data are imposed outside the interior grid. With nodal white noise represented as $h^{-1}\xi$, $\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$, a discrete SPDE model has covariance
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma
    =
    \sigma^2 h^{-2}
    A^{-1}
    (I+VCV^\top)
    A^{-T},
    \label{eq:discrete_covariance_design}
\end{equation}
where the spike term is absent unless source variability is included. The assembled finite-difference operators are symmetric in the present study, so $A^{-T}=A^{-1}$ numerically; we retain the transpose notation in discrete covariance formulas to make the covariance construction explicit. This is the finite-difference counterpart of the continuum construction in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\subsection{Replicates and resolution regimes}
\label{sec:design_regimes}

Each main fit uses $n_{\rm train}=200$ independent zero-mean training fields and $n_{\rm test}=100$ independent test fields; the realizations are not sample-centred. Model fitting is performed at $n_x\in\{16,32\}$, while the refinement diagnostic is evaluated at $n_x\in\{16,24,32,48,64\}$. The latter sequence deliberately increases the aspect ratio $\gamma=N/n_{\rm train}$ while holding the ensemble size fixed, thereby testing whether the diagnostic remains informative as covariance estimation becomes increasingly high dimensional. Table~\ref{tab:regimes} reports the resulting Marchenko--Pastur edge and the number of grid cells resolving each localized feature. In particular, the well width is smaller than one mesh spacing at the coarsest grid, so refinement changes both the statistical aspect ratio and the spatial resolution of the source profiles. Consequently, spike strengths estimated at $n_x=16$ are not interpreted as directly comparable discretizations of the finer-grid source amplitudes, and the observed decay of source-only refinement statistics begins from an under-resolved spatial representation.

For the sample-based refinement experiment, five independently generated training ensembles of 200 fields are used at each truth and resolution. The main model-comparison data use the deterministic seed rule in Table~\ref{tab:setup}; additional seeds are reserved for robustness checks.

\subsection{Oracle refinement quantities and predictions}
\label{sec:oracle_design}

Because the synthetic population covariance is known, the first experiment evaluates the population relative covariance directly,
\begin{equation}
    T_N
    =
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}
    \Sigma_{1,N}
    \Sigma_{0,N}^{-1/2}-I_N,
\end{equation}
without sampling error. The oracle calculations record the relative spectrum, the Hilbert--Schmidt energy $D_N=\|T_N\|_F^2$, the normalized energy $D_N/N$, and the Gaussian divergence $\mathrm{KL}_N(\mu_1\|\mu_0)$. They also record the diagonal quantity
\begin{equation}
    h_N^{\rm oracle}
    =
    \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{ii}^2,
    \label{eq:oracle_hN_design}
\end{equation}
which is the population target of the sample statistic in Section~\ref{sec:diag_stat}.

For the configured smooth conductivity lens, the local-symbol prediction is
\begin{equation}
    h_\infty
    =
    \frac{1}{|\Omega|}
    \int_\Omega\left[K(x)^{-2}-1\right]^2\,dx
    =0.060732.
    \label{eq:hinfty_design}
\end{equation}
Midpoint quadrature from $256^2$ through $2048^2$ points agrees at the reported precision. The exact discrete oracle values for $n_x=16,24,32,48,64$ are, respectively,
\begin{equation}
    h_N^{\rm oracle}
    =
    (0.06380,\;0.06363,\;0.06325,\;0.06266,\;0.06228),
    \label{eq:oracle_h_sequence}
\end{equation}
and approach the continuum prediction $h_\infty=0.060732$ from above. At the finest configured grid the discrete value remains approximately $2.5\%$ above the limiting value. Accordingly, the sample statistic $\widehat h_N$ is compared with $h_N^{\rm oracle}$ at each finite grid, while $h_\infty$ is used only as the asymptotic target. These quantities depend only on the configured truth and reference and are computed before any sample-based fitting.

\subsection{Sample diagnostic protocol}
\label{sec:sample_protocol}

At each truth and resolution, M1 is first fitted to the 200 training fields and used as the plug-in whitening reference. The whitened sample covariance is then analyzed at two scales. Positive isolated directions are counted using the unmodified Marchenko--Pastur upper edge
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_+
    =
    (1+\sqrt{N/n_{\rm train}})^2,
\end{equation}
with multiplicative edge factor $1.0$, i.e. no finite-sample safety margin above the asymptotic edge. As a consequence, an isolated eigenvalue may occasionally cross the threshold even under the reference population; the outlier count is therefore interpreted as a diagnostic rather than a formal level-controlled test. When positive outliers are removed before evaluating the diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N$, the nodal leverage correction in \eqref{eq:deflated_di} uses a denominator floor of $10^{-3}$. The finite-sample variance term in \eqref{eq:Hhat} is subtracted with the factor $2/(n+2)$ using the actual number of fields in the evaluated ensemble.

The principal diagnostic is the diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N=\widehat H_N/N$, with and without positive-spike deflation, compared against the oracle target \eqref{eq:oracle_hN_design}. Because both the fitted reference and deflation directions are estimated from data, an additional cross-fitted analysis estimates these quantities on one subset of fields and evaluates $\widehat h_N$ on an independent subset. The sign-specific deflation rule is used only for the stiff-lens setting $K\ge1$ studied here; the diagonal refinement statistic $h_N$ itself is sign insensitive.

\subsection{Model-fitting protocol}
\label{sec:model_fit_design}

All covariance models defined in Section~\ref{sec:model_classes} are fitted to the same training ensembles. Table~\ref{tab:models} records the parameterizations, initializations, basis sizes, and optimization schemes. M1 is initialized at the reference values and optimized without bounds on $\kappa$. M2 uses six cubic B-splines in $\log\lambda$ as a flexible stationary control. M3-joint searches ranks $0$ through $8$ by five-fold held-out likelihood and alternates between the stationary base and a positive-semidefinite spike correction; in its PCA step the whitened baseline noise variance is fixed at one.

M4-$\kappa$ and M4-$K$ use 25 Gaussian radial basis functions centred on a $5\times5$ grid in $\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}^2$, each with width $0.12$. Their coefficient penalty is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_\theta\|\theta\|_2^2,
    \qquad
    \lambda_\theta=0.01,
\end{equation}
added to the total training negative log-likelihood. The value is fixed by hand rather than selected by cross-validation; because the likelihood scales with both the number of fields and the field dimension, this penalty is small relative to the data term. All held-out scores are computed from the unpenalized likelihood.

M5 searches ranks $0$ through $5$ by the same five-fold criterion but differs from M3 in its spike update. Given the current operator, M5 applies the PPCA closed form with a free residual variance, jointly updating $\sigma^2$, the positive spike strengths, and their directions. Given that spike block, the principal-operator coefficients are updated by L-BFGS. The block coordinate-ascent cycle is monotone up to numerical tolerance and stops on a per-cycle objective tolerance. Every M5 fit begins from a flat conductivity field rather than the corresponding M4-$K$ solution.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}

Models are evaluated on the same 100 held-out fields generated independently from each truth. The primary population score is the full-field Gaussian log likelihood. For presentation, each fitted model is paired with the corresponding truth model on the same test fields and reported as a log-score difference in nats per field, so zero is the population-model benchmark.

A second evaluation conditions on ten noisy point observations. Let $H$ select the sensor nodes and
\begin{equation}
    y=Hu+\varepsilon,
    \qquad
    \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_y^2I),
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_y$ equal to $5\%$ of the reference-field RMS. For each held-out field we record the conditional log predictive density of the full field, $\log p(u_{\rm test}\mid y)$, and the RMSE of the corresponding Gaussian posterior mean. These sensor-conditioned scores are reported separately from the primary full-field model-comparison tables in Section~5; they test whether a learned prior remains useful when combined with sparse observations rather than evaluating covariance fit alone.

Mechanism recovery is evaluated separately from predictive score. For B and C we record the correlation between the fitted and true $\log K$ fields together with the fitted range parameter $\widehat\kappa$. For A and C we record the selected spike rank, estimated spike strengths, per-direction cosines, and principal-angle cosines between the estimated and true three-dimensional source subspaces. Refinement diagnostics are reported through $D_N$, $D_N/N$, $\mathrm{KL}_N$, $h_N^{\rm oracle}$, and $\widehat h_N$.

\subsection{Robustness analyses and chronology}
\label{sec:robustness_design}

The main comparisons are supplemented by targeted robustness analyses. Model rankings are repeated for three additional independently generated training/test seeds. The conductivity fit is repeated after moving the smooth lens centre from $(0.5,0.5)$ to $(0.42,0.57)$ so that it no longer coincides with an RBF centre. The oracle refinement calculation is repeated for a discontinuous disc lens and for a constant principal coefficient $K\equiv1.5$. Additional checks vary the operator penalty $\lambda_\theta$, use cross-fitted whitening and deflation in the sample diagnostic, vary the replicate count for the weak B$'$ leakage case, and compare the exact M5 block-coordinate implementation with the earlier legacy update. All main M5 results use the exact block-coordinate implementation; the legacy fit is retained only as a comparison in the robustness analysis.

The sequence of analyses is also recorded explicitly. The physical departures and oracle refinement predictions were specified before the sample-based diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N$ and M5 were developed. The latter were introduced subsequently to address the finite-ensemble classification problem and the mixed source/operator population, and are evaluated separately from the oracle calculations.

\section{Results: refinement behavior and mechanism attribution}
\label{sec:results}

The numerical results are organized around four questions. First, does mesh refinement recover the distinction predicted by the continuum theory when the population covariance is known exactly? Second, can the same distinction be recovered from only 200 replicated fields? Third, what happens when a covariance departure is forced into the wrong structural component? Finally, does the combined model use its operator and finite-rank components selectively when one or both mechanisms are present? The emphasis is therefore on mechanism attribution rather than on predictive ranking alone.

\subsection{Oracle refinement separates finite-rank, lower-order, and principal-part departures}
\label{sec:results_oracle}

Figure~\ref{fig:F5_1} and Table~\ref{tab:T5_1} first remove sampling uncertainty and compare the exact relative covariance under refinement. These oracle refinement signatures were specified as predictions before the sample-based diagnostic study. Truth A behaves exactly as a finite-rank perturbation of the reference. Its Hilbert--Schmidt energy and Gaussian divergence are independent of resolution,
\begin{equation}
    D_N=13300,
    \qquad
    \mathrm{KL}_N=88.83,
\end{equation}
while $D_N/N$ decreases as the number of resolved degrees of freedom grows. The diagonal energy also decreases rapidly with refinement. The coarse-grid values should not be interpreted as an asymptotic rate because the source profiles are under-resolved at $n_x=16$; the relevant structural observation is that the total relative covariance energy remains bounded as $N$ increases.

The lower-order leakage perturbation B$'$ has the same qualitative refinement signature without being finite rank. Its total energy approaches a finite value,
\begin{equation}
    D_N: 0.613\longrightarrow0.586,
\end{equation}
and its divergence approaches approximately $0.109$ nats. Consequently $D_N/N\rightarrow0$, while the oracle diagonal energy falls from $5.8\times10^{-4}$ to $5.0\times10^{-6}$. This is the numerical signature expected of an infinite-rank but Hilbert--Schmidt departure.

The conductivity lens B behaves differently. Its relative covariance energy is extensive, meaning that $D_N$ grows linearly with $N$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{D_N}{N}
    =
    0.0646,\ 0.0640,\ 0.0635,\ 0.0628,\ 0.0623
\end{equation}
over the five grids. The KL divergence grows nearly exactly linearly with the number of degrees of freedom,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{KL}_N
    \approx
    0.16+0.0315N,
\end{equation}
with log--log slope $1.001$. The local statistic provides an independent view of the same principal-part structure: the exact diagonal energy decreases from $0.06380$ to $0.06228$ and approaches the symbol prediction $h_\infty=0.060732$ from above. Thus the smooth conductivity perturbation realizes the persistent-mode refinement behavior discussed in Section~\ref{sec:refinement_criterion}, rather than merely violating equivalence abstractly.

Table~\ref{tab:T5_1} also checks the two quantitative predictions of Lemma~\ref{lem:mult_compact}. First, $D_N/N$ and $h_N$ converge to each other: their gap is $8.3\times10^{-4}$, $4.0\times10^{-4}$, $2.3\times10^{-4}$, $1.1\times10^{-4}$ and $6\times10^{-5}$ over the five grids (from $1.3\%$ to $0.1\%$ of $h_N$), so the off-diagonal relative covariance energy per node vanishes, as it must for multiplication plus a compact remainder. Second, the lemma predicts the KL slope
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{2|\mathcal D|}\int_{\mathcal D}\big[m-\log(1+m)\big]\,dx=0.030796
\end{equation}
for the lens. The exact values $\mathrm{KL}_N/N=0.0314,\ 0.0318,\ 0.0318,\ 0.0316,\ 0.0315$ lie $2$--$3\%$ above it, the same relative offset as $h_N$ above $h_\infty$ ($2.5\%$ at $n_x=64$), so the fitted slope $0.0315$ is a prediction rather than a fit.

The $L^2$-Wasserstein distance provides a useful contrast. For B it remains only about $4\times10^{-3}$ across the same refinement sequence even while $\mathrm{KL}_N$ grows from $8.0$ to $129.0$ nats. The fields can therefore remain close in an ordinary $L^2$ transport metric while their covariance ratio accumulates an extensive high-frequency discrepancy. Truth C superposes the two mechanisms: at the available resolutions its $D_N/N$ is dominated by the large finite-rank source component and decreases with $N$, while its KL divergence grows from $87.6$ to $208.8$ nats and its diagonal energy approaches the lens contribution from above. The different summaries therefore separate the finite-dimensional and distributed parts of the mixed departure.

\subsection{The refinement distinction survives finite sampling}
\label{sec:results_sample}

The sample experiment replaces the exact population covariance by 200 replicated fields and whitens them with a fitted stationary M1. The diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N$ was developed after the oracle refinement results to ask whether the same distinction could be recovered without access to the population covariance, as recorded in Section~\ref{sec:robustness_design}. Figure~\ref{fig:F5_2} and Table~\ref{tab:T5_2} show that the two-scale diagnostic retains the oracle distinction despite both finite-sample covariance noise and reference fitting.

Under the reference population, $\widehat h_N$ fluctuates around zero at all resolutions. The use of the unmodified Marchenko--Pastur edge occasionally produces one apparent upper outlier: the mean count is $0.2$ at $n_x=32$ and $48$. This is consistent with the role assigned to $r_{\rm MP}$ in Section~\ref{sec:sample_protocol}: it is an outlier diagnostic, not a level-controlled hypothesis test.

Truth A gives a qualitatively different spectral signal. Exactly three upper outliers are detected in every replicate at every resolution. Before deflation the diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N$ is dominated by the localized source directions. After removing those directions, however,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat h_N^{\rm defl}
    =
    0.1440,\ 0.0445,\ 0.0173,\ 0.0050,\ 0.0023,
\end{equation}
and tends toward zero under refinement. This occurs even though the stationary whitening fit drives $\widehat\kappa$ toward the boundary, from approximately $1.6\times10^{-3}$ at the coarsest grid to values of order $10^{-4}$ on the finer grids. The plug-in reference can therefore be badly distorted in its low-frequency range parameter without producing a persistent fine-scale diagonal plateau.

The weak leakage case B$'$ is also consistent with an in-class departure. It produces no stable outlier count and both raw and deflated $\widehat h_N$ fluctuate around zero at the finer grids. In contrast, B remains nearly resolution invariant. The raw statistic is
\begin{equation}
    0.0617,\ 0.0624,\ 0.0623,\ 0.0612,\ 0.0612,
\end{equation}
while the exact finite-grid targets are
\begin{equation}
    0.06380,\ 0.06363,\ 0.06325,\ 0.06266,\ 0.06228.
\end{equation}
For the stiff lens $K\ge1$, the leading principal-part contribution to the lens block of $T$ is non-positive, so the positive directions deflated on B are occasional finite-sample upper-edge crossings rather than lens signal. After removing them, the estimate lies between $0.0604$ and $0.0611$, close to the independently specified continuum value $h_\infty=0.060732$. Thus the persistent principal-part signature is visible from replicated fields without direct access to $\Sigma_1$.

The mixed truth C provides the more stringent test because the fitted stationary reference again collapses its range and the raw statistic is dominated by three source directions. Nevertheless, three upper outliers are recovered in every replicate; unlike B, these are the three genuine source directions. After their removal
\begin{equation}
    \widehat h_N^{\rm defl}
    =
    0.1702,\ 0.0835,\ 0.0651,\ 0.0585,\ 0.0582.
\end{equation}
The sequence approaches the same lens-scale plateau seen under B rather than tending to zero as under A. In other words, source deflation exposes the distributed principal-part component that remains underneath the finite-dimensional covariance spikes. Because the deflation directions are estimated from the same fields, the absolute values are interpreted as a bias-corrected diagnostic rather than an exactly unbiased estimator; the refinement pattern is the principal result.

\subsection{Wrong-class models fit by reallocating variability to the wrong mechanism}
\label{sec:results_misattribution}

The previous subsections identify the population structures before fitting flexible covariance models. The model comparison asks a different question: when a model class cannot represent the true mechanism directly, which of its available components absorbs the discrepancy? Figure~\ref{fig:F5_3} and Tables~\ref{tab:T5_3}--\ref{tab:T5_4b} show that these failures are systematic rather than simple losses in predictive score.

For the source-only truth A, the stationary model absorbs the low-frequency effect of the wells by driving its range parameter to the boundary. At $n_x=16$ and $32$,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat\kappa_{\rm M1}
    =
    7.2\times10^{-5},
    \quad
    3.0\times10^{-5},
\end{equation}
instead of the generating value $\kappa_0=6$. Giving the model a flexible stationary spectrum improves held-out likelihood but does not repair this mechanism attribution: M2 follows the same collapsed range. Allowing conductivity variation without source spikes leads to a second form of misattribution. This explicit wrong-operator attribution check was added after the oracle refinement results. M4-$K$ places spatial conductivity structure around the well locations while again collapsing $\widehat\kappa$ to approximately $10^{-4}$; its held-out deficit remains $31.1$ and $47.4$ nats per field at the two resolutions.

Joint source correction resolves both failures. M3-joint selects rank three, returns $\widehat\kappa=6.18$ and $6.21$, and improves the held-out scores to $-1.76$ and $-5.78$ nats per field, corresponding to deficits of $1.76$ and $5.78$ nats per field. Fitting the stationary reference first and adding spikes only afterward is substantially worse. M3-nested inherits the collapsed reference and consequently assigns smaller spike strengths to the wells. The generating strengths are $90/60/40$; at $n_x=32$ the nested fit gives $63.1/47.2/29.9$, whereas the joint fit gives $101.8/77.3/47.6$. Thus the nested fit underestimates all three strengths, while the joint fit overshoots them. The latter is consistent with BBP-type upward sample-eigenvalue inflation in finite-sample spiked covariance models and should not be interpreted as an operator-fit failure. The corresponding source-subspace cosines are also weaker for the nested fit. This demonstrates that a correction can be structurally appropriate yet still be distorted if the reference is estimated independently and allowed to absorb the same variation first.

The converse failure appears under the principal-part truth B. Neither M1, M2, nor M3-joint can represent the conductivity field, and CV correctly selects $r=0$ for M3. Their held-out deficit grows from about $7.0$ nats per field at $n_x=16$ to $29.0$ at $n_x=32$. Since the number of degrees of freedom increases by a factor of four, the score deficit grows by a factor of $4.14$, matching the extensive refinement signature seen in the oracle KL calculation. M4-$K$, by contrast, remains near the truth at both resolutions, with held-out scores $-0.094$ and $-0.120$ nats per field (deficits $0.094$ and $0.120$) and $\mathrm{corr}(\log\widehat K,\log K_{\rm true})$ increasing from $0.997$ to $0.999$.

Allowing only a spatially varying lower-order term does not solve this mismatch. M4-$\kappa$ improves the score only modestly and deforms its coefficient field dramatically: at $n_x=32$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\kappa(x)^2}{\widehat\kappa^2}
    \in[1.3,857].
\end{equation}
The optimization audit confirms that this is a converged fit rather than a premature optimizer stop. It is therefore a concrete example of a lower-order model using extreme coefficient variation to imitate a principal-part change that it cannot represent directly.

The weak leakage truth B$'$ provides the complementary control. M4-$\kappa$ is the structurally matched model and is the only model with a score indistinguishable from the truth at both resolutions, but all B$'$ score differences are small relative to their paired standard errors. The experiment therefore supports the structural classification more strongly through refinement than through predictive ranking in this deliberately weak case.

Finally, the mixed truth C exposes the cost of forcing two mechanisms into either component alone. At $n_x=32$, M3-joint captures the three wells but leaves a held-out score of $-34.97$ nats per field, i.e. a deficit of $34.97$, because the lens remains unmodeled. M4-$K$ instead tries to absorb the source variation into the operator: $\widehat\kappa$ collapses to $1.1\times10^{-4}$ and the conductivity-shape correlation reaches only $0.886$, despite a peak $\widehat K$ close to the true amplitude. Predictive adequacy alone would therefore obscure the structural source of the error; the fitted parameters show which mechanism each wrong-class model is using to compensate.

\subsection{The combined model separates source and operator variability}
\label{sec:results_m5}

M5 is intended to prevent the compensating failures above by allowing the principal operator and the finite-rank correction to be learned together. The combined architecture was developed after the oracle results exposed the need to represent source and principal-part departures simultaneously, as recorded in Section~\ref{sec:robustness_design}. Figure~\ref{fig:F5_4} shows that the two components are used selectively rather than both being activated indiscriminately. In the diagnostic control at $n_x=32$, deflation removed $1.2\pm0.4$ upper-edge directions on B, where they are sampling fluctuations, and exactly $3.0\pm0.0$ on C, where they are the three source directions.

For truth A at $n_x=32$, CV selects $r=3$, the range returns to $\widehat\kappa=6.08$, and the fitted conductivity remains nearly flat. On this same dataset, M1 alone gives $\widehat\kappa=3.0\times10^{-5}$: allowing the source component therefore moves the inferred range by roughly five orders of magnitude back to the generating value $\kappa_0=6$. Its held-out score, $-5.86$ nats per field, is essentially the same as M3-joint ($-5.78$), showing that adding an operator component does not force spurious conductivity structure when the source correction already explains the population variation. The recovered source subspace is also nearly identical to the M3-joint result: its three principal-angle cosines are $0.973/0.963/0.941$.

For B, M5 takes the opposite special case. CV selects $r=0$, so the finite-rank component switches off. The fit recovers
\begin{equation}
    \widehat\kappa=5.99,
    \qquad
    \mathrm{corr}(\log\widehat K,\log K_{\rm true})=0.999,
    \qquad
    \max\widehat K=2.67,
\end{equation}
against the generating values $\kappa_0=6$ and $\max K=e=2.718$. Its held-out score is numerically identical to M4-$K$, as expected when no source correction is required.

The mixed truth C requires both components. At $n_x=32$, M5 selects $r=3$, recovers
\begin{equation}
    \widehat\kappa=6.06,
    \qquad
    \mathrm{corr}(\log\widehat K,\log K_{\rm true})=0.999,
    \qquad
    \max\widehat K=2.61,
\end{equation}
and retains a three-dimensional source subspace with principal-angle cosines $0.975/0.954/0.946$ relative to the true well span. The source strengths are estimated as $104.5/62.0/48.5$ compared with the generating values $90/60/40$. The corresponding held-out score is $-6.37$ nats per field (deficit $6.37$), compared with scores of $-34.97$ for M3-joint and $-42.24$ for M4-$K$. The gain is therefore not simply due to additional flexibility: the fitted decomposition simultaneously restores the reference range, the conductivity shape, and the finite-dimensional source subspace that each single-component model confounds.

M5 is not intended to replace the lower-order model. On B$'$, CV selects $r=0$ and the conductivity field remains close to flat, but M5 does not contain a spatially varying leakage coefficient; M4-$\kappa$ remains the structurally matched model. This distinction is important for interpreting M5 as a source-plus-principal architecture rather than as a universal covariance approximator.

The exact block-coordinate implementation used for the main results also leaves the scientific conclusion unchanged relative to the earlier legacy alternation. On C at $n_x=32$, the held-out score changes only from $-6.377$ to $-6.373$ nats per field and the conductivity correlation remains $0.9994$, while $\widehat\kappa$ moves from $5.95$ to $6.06$. Similar score differences are negligible on A, B$'$, and B. The optimization correction therefore affects the internal consistency of the fitted decomposition more than the substantive model comparison.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}

Across the main seed and three fresh seeds at $n_x=32$, M5 gives held-out scores $-7.04\pm0.86$, $-0.10\pm0.02$, and $-7.14\pm0.50$ nats per field on A, B, and C and selects ranks $3$, $0$, and $3$, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:r-seeds}), so the M5 allocation on C is unchanged and the seed perturbation does not alter the headline $h_\infty$ plateau on B or the $\widehat\kappa$ drift on A.

Moving the lens centre off the RBF grid to $(0.42,0.57)$ increases the $n_x=32$ M5 deficits to $1.73$ nats per field on B and $7.91$ on C and reduces the lens correlation to approximately $0.974$, but M5 still selects $r=0$ on B and $r=3$ on C (Table~\ref{tab:r-seeds}), so the allocation headline is unchanged and neither the B plateau nor the A range-drift conclusion is altered by basis placement.

For the discontinuous disc lens, $D_N/N$ remains non-vanishing at $0.0351$ and $\mathrm{KL}_N$ reaches $104.30$ nats by $n_x=64$ (Table~\ref{tab:r-disc}), so the principal-part refinement signature persists beyond the smooth lens and leaves the smooth-B $h_\infty$ plateau, A range drift, and C allocation headlines unchanged.

For the constant rescaling $K\equiv1.5$, $D_N/N$ approaches $0.3070$ and $\mathrm{KL}_N$ reaches $519.22$ nats at $n_x=64$ (Table~\ref{tab:r-disc}), confirming extensive principal-part behavior under a global coefficient change and leaving the three headline conclusions unchanged.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central result is that replicated fields can contain enough information to decide whether a discrepancy from a Gaussian reference should be represented as a correction of that reference or as a change in the covariance-generating operator. In the controlled SPDE study, finite-dimensional source variability and lower-order leakage produced refinement behavior consistent with the reference measure class, whereas a principal-coefficient change produced persistent relative covariance energy and an extensive KL cost. The diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N$ converted this continuum distinction into a sample quantity: for the conductivity lens, its finite-grid values tracked the oracle sequence and approached the independently predicted constant $h_\infty=0.060732$. The same distinction appeared in model fitting. Wrong-class models did not merely lose predictive score; they reassigned variability to physically plausible but incorrect components. Source variability drove a stationary fit from the generating $\kappa_0=6$ to $\widehat\kappa\approx3\times10^{-5}$, while a lower-order or correction-only model distorted itself to imitate principal-part variation. When both source and operator variability were available simultaneously, M5 allocated them to separate components and recovered the reference range, conductivity field, and source subspace.

The resulting modeling rule is therefore: \emph{learn the operator where the population demands a change of Gaussian measure class; correct relative to that operator where the remaining discrepancy stays within class}. The rule is structural rather than tied to the aquifer example. A low-rank or Hilbert--Schmidt correction is appropriate when refinement reveals a bounded relative perturbation. When a discrepancy persists across an increasing fraction of resolved modes, adding more correction terms to a fixed reference does not address the underlying mismatch; the reference itself must be changed.

\paragraph{Implications for learned priors and function-space generative models.}
Methods intended to define probability measures independently of discretization require a reference whose continuum geometry is compatible with the target population. Function-space generative constructions already make absolute-continuity and Cameron--Martin assumptions explicit \citep{Lim2025,Pidstrigach2023}. The present results add a practical population-learning consequence: if the principal part of the Gaussian reference is wrong, no finite-dimensional correction can repair the measure-class mismatch, even though a fixed mesh can make the corrected covariance appear adequate. The failure becomes visible only as resolution increases. A refinement diagnostic of the type used here can therefore serve as a pre-training check on whether a chosen Gaussian reference is structurally adequate before a more flexible model is learned around it.

\paragraph{Implications for reference-based complexity priors.}
PC priors quantify complexity as departure from a chosen base model \citep{Simpson2017,Fuglstad2019}. For a principal-part mismatch, the finite-dimensional Gaussian divergence in this study grows as $\mathrm{KL}_N=O(N)$, so the corresponding KL distance
\begin{equation}
    d_N=\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{KL}_N}
\end{equation}
scales as $O(\sqrt N)$. The same physical departure therefore appears increasingly complex as the mesh is refined. A discretization-stable notion of complexity in this setting requires either a different distance, as in approaches developed for singular operator changes \citep{LlamazaresElias2026}, or a new base operator with respect to which the remaining departure is within class. This is an upstream issue to regularization: before asking how strongly to shrink toward a reference, one must ask whether that reference belongs to the right continuum class.

\paragraph{Implications for spatial covariance fitting.}
The source-only experiment provides a concrete warning about interpreting fitted range parameters. With the wells present, the stationary fit gives $\widehat\kappa\approx3\times10^{-5}$ even though the generating value is $6$; once source variability is represented explicitly, M5 returns $\widehat\kappa=6.08$ on the same truth. For the range component of the standard Mat\'ern PC construction, the base corresponds to infinite range, or $\kappa=0$ \citep{Fuglstad2019}. In this example, shrinkage toward that base would therefore reinforce the confounded direction selected by the misspecified stationary model rather than correct it. A near-zero fitted range in the presence of unmodeled sources should consequently be treated as a possible mechanism-attribution warning, not automatically as evidence for truly long-range spatial dependence.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Several robustness checks in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reduce implementation-specific concerns: although $\lambda_\theta$ was fixed by hand, sweeping it from $10^{-3}$ to $10$ leaves the B and C M5 ranks and scores unchanged at the reported precision; across four seeds the $n_x=32$ M5 ranks remain $3/0/3$ for A/B/C; moving the lens off the RBF grid lowers the fitted lens correlation to about $0.974$ but preserves the B/C rank allocation; a discontinuous disc lens retains non-vanishing $D_N/N$ and a growing KL cost; and the constant rescaling $K\equiv1.5$ likewise retains extensive principal-part scaling. The remaining limitations are structural. The source-deflation rule was evaluated with a stiff conductivity lens, $K\ge1$, so although the diagonal refinement statistic $h_N$ is sign insensitive, the upper-edge source-removal step is not yet a general soft-lens classifier. The study is two-dimensional and employs a square domain whose corners fall outside the smooth-boundary assumptions of the strongest variable-coefficient equivalence theorem. M5 does not include a spatially varying lower-order coefficient, M4 fits use a single initialization, and auxiliary Anderson--Paul-type corrections are applied beyond the regime in which all finite-sample assumptions are rigorously controlled. No real aquifer data are analyzed, and the sample-based diagonal refinement statistic $\widehat h_N$ and M5 architecture were developed after the oracle refinement results rather than specified in advance.

\paragraph{Next steps.}
The immediate extensions follow directly from these boundaries. A soft-lens experiment would separate the sign-insensitive refinement diagnostic from the sign-specific deflation rule, and low-replicate regimes should be studied to determine how much population replication is needed before the refinement classification becomes reliable. Lemma~\ref{lem:mult_compact} should be extended to discontinuous principal coefficients, as the disc-lens computation suggests, to tensor-valued $K$, and to $d=3$. A second direction is computational: principal-part hyperparameters generate mutually singular continuum families, so the behavior of centered and noncentered samplers under mesh refinement deserves direct study. The synthetic aquifer study should ultimately be tested against replicated observations from a pumped heterogeneous aquifer. More broadly, the same question arises in mechanics when a learned population prior over constitutive or hardening behavior is transferred to a new experiment: one must determine whether the new population is a correction around the learned reference or whether the operator or constitutive representation defining that reference must change.

The opening question can therefore be answered empirically: whether to correct a Gaussian reference or change its operator is a question that replicated data can answer, and getting that decision wrong can produce a confident, physically plausible, but structurally incorrect model.

\section{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:refinement}}
\label{app:proof_refinement}

\begin{proof}
If $T\in\mathcal S_2$ and $P_N\to I$ strongly, then finite-rank compression converges in Hilbert--Schmidt norm,
\begin{equation}
    \|P_NTP_N-T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
    D_N
    =
    \|P_NTP_N\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
    \longrightarrow
    \|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2<\infty.
\end{equation}
Since the numerator converges to a finite limit while $N\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{D_N}{N}\longrightarrow0. \qedhere
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:mult_compact}}
\label{app:proof_lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $\mathcal K$ for the compact operators on $L^2(\mathcal D)$.

\emph{Step 1 (regularity).} For a smooth or convex polygonal domain and $K\in C^1(\overline{\mathcal D})$, $K\ge k_0>0$, both $A_0^{-1}$ and $A_1^{-1}$ map $L^2(\mathcal D)$ boundedly into $H^2(\mathcal D)\cap H^1_0(\mathcal D)$. Hence $A_0A_1^{-1}$ and $A_1A_0^{-1}$ extend to bounded operators on $L^2(\mathcal D)$, and each is the inverse of the other.

\emph{Step 2 ($A_1A_0^{-1}=M_K+\mathcal K$).} Let $B=A_1-A_0=-\nabla\!\cdot((K-1)\nabla)=-(K-1)\Delta-\nabla K\cdot\nabla$. Since $-\Delta A_0^{-1}=I-\kappa^2A_0^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}
    BA_0^{-1}=M_{K-1}-\kappa^2M_{K-1}A_0^{-1}-(\nabla K\cdot\nabla)A_0^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The operator $A_0^{-1}$ is compact on $L^2$, and $\nabla A_0^{-1}$ maps $L^2$ boundedly into $H^1$, which embeds compactly into $L^2$ (Rellich). Multiplication by the bounded functions $K-1$ and $\nabla K$ preserves compactness. Hence $BA_0^{-1}=M_{K-1}+C_1$ with $C_1\in\mathcal K$, and $A_1A_0^{-1}=I+BA_0^{-1}=M_K+C_1$.

\emph{Step 3 ($A_0A_1^{-1}=M_{1/K}+\mathcal K$).} Let $G=A_0A_1^{-1}$. From $(M_K+C_1)G=I$,
\begin{equation}
    G=M_{1/K}-M_{1/K}C_1G=M_{1/K}+C_2,
    \qquad
    C_2\in\mathcal K.
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 4 (the decomposition).} Since $\Sigma_0^{-1/2}=c^{-1/2}A_0$ and $\Sigma_1=c\,A_1^{-2}$, and $A_0,A_1$ are self-adjoint,
\begin{equation}
    T+I=A_0A_1^{-1}A_1^{-1}A_0=GG^*=(M_{1/K}+C_2)(M_{1/K}+C_2^*)=M_{1/K^2}+C_3,
    \qquad
    C_3\in\mathcal K,
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{eq:mult_compact} with $R=C_3$. Since $G$ is invertible, $T+I\ge\gamma I$ for some $\gamma>0$.

\emph{(i).} By Weyl's theorem, compact perturbations do not change the essential spectrum, so $\sigma_{\rm ess}(T)=\sigma_{\rm ess}(M_m)$, the essential range of $m$, which equals $m(\overline{\mathcal D})$ for continuous $m$. A compact operator has essential spectrum $\{0\}$, so $T$ is compact only if $m\equiv0$, i.e.\ $K\equiv1$.

\emph{(ii).} In the orthonormal basis of normalized cell indicators, $P_NM_mP_N$ is diagonal with entries the cell averages $\bar m_i$, so $\frac1N\|P_NM_mP_N\|_{\rm HS}^2=\frac1N\sum_i\bar m_i^2\to|\mathcal D|^{-1}\int m^2$ by uniform continuity of $m$. For the remainder, $P_NRP_N$ has rank at most $N$ and singular values $s_j(P_NRP_N)\le s_j(R)$, so
\begin{equation}
    \frac1N\|P_NRP_N\|_{\rm HS}^2\le\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}s_j(R)^2\longrightarrow0,
\end{equation}
because $s_j(R)\to0$ and Ces\`aro means of a null sequence vanish. The cross term vanishes by Cauchy--Schwarz, giving the first limit in \eqref{eq:lemma_energy}. The diagonal entries of $T_N$ are $\bar m_i+(P_NRP_N)_{ii}$ with $\frac1N\sum_i(P_NRP_N)_{ii}^2\le\frac1N\|P_NRP_N\|_{\rm HS}^2\to0$, giving the second limit. Because $P_NM_mP_N$ has no off-diagonal entries in this basis, the difference of the two quantities tends to zero.

\emph{(iii).} By the Hoffman--Wielandt inequality, with eigenvalues and cell averages both sorted,
\begin{equation}
    \frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|t_{j,N}-\bar m_{(j)}\big|^2\le\frac1N\|P_NRP_N\|_{\rm HS}^2\longrightarrow0,
\end{equation}
so the empirical eigenvalue distribution of $T_N$ has the same weak limit as that of the $\bar m_i$, namely the law of $m(X)$. The persistent-mode statement follows at every $\delta$ with $|\{|m|=\delta\}|=0$. Finally, all $t_{j,N}$ and $\bar m_i$ lie in a compact interval $[\gamma'-1,\|T\|]$ with $\gamma'=\min(\gamma,\min K^{-2})>0$, on which $f(t)=t-\log(1+t)$ is Lipschitz; hence $\frac1N\sum_jf(t_{j,N})-\frac1N\sum_if(\bar m_i)\to0$, and the second sum tends to $|\mathcal D|^{-1}\int f(m)$. This gives \eqref{eq:lemma_kl}.
\end{proof}
